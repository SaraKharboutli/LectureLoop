Today we are talking about photosynthesis. And I know some of you are thinking, I learned this in high school, plants make food from sunlight, done. But by the end of today I want you to understand how that actually happens, step by step, because it is going to come up again and again in this course.

So let's start with the big picture. Photosynthesis is the process plants, algae, and some bacteria use to convert light energy into chemical energy. That chemical energy is stored in sugar molecules, mainly glucose. The overall equation is carbon dioxide plus water, using light energy, gives you glucose and oxygen. So the inputs are carbon dioxide and water, and the outputs are sugar and oxygen. Keep that big picture in mind: light energy in, chemical energy stored in sugar out.

Now, where does this happen? It happens inside a structure called the chloroplast. The chloroplast has an outer membrane and an inner membrane, and inside it there are stacks of flattened discs called thylakoids. A stack of thylakoids is called a granum. The fluid that surrounds the thylakoids is called the stroma. The green pigment chlorophyll sits in the thylakoid membranes, and that is where light is absorbed. So remember the two locations, because they matter: the thylakoid membranes, and the stroma around them.

My grandmother used to talk to her tomato plants every morning. She swore it made them grow faster. I did not have the heart to tell her that it was probably the extra carbon dioxide from all that talking. Anyway.

Photosynthesis has two main stages. The first stage is the light-dependent reactions, and the second stage is the Calvin cycle, which some textbooks call the light-independent reactions. Let's take them one at a time.

The light-dependent reactions happen in the thylakoid membranes. Chlorophyll absorbs light, and that energy excites electrons. Those high-energy electrons move along an electron transport chain. To replace the electrons that chlorophyll loses, water molecules are split. And when water is split, oxygen is released as a by-product. So the oxygen we breathe comes from water, not from carbon dioxide. That surprises a lot of people. The energy from the moving electrons is used to make two molecules: ATP and NADPH. ATP is the energy currency, and NADPH carries high-energy electrons. So to summarize the light-dependent reactions: they use light to split water, release oxygen, and produce ATP and NADPH, which are the energy carriers for the next stage.

Okay, so now we move to the second stage, the Calvin cycle. The Calvin cycle happens in the stroma, not in the thylakoids. This is where carbon dioxide from the air is actually turned into sugar. The key enzyme here is called rubisco. Rubisco attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon molecule called RuBP. This step is called carbon fixation, because we are fixing carbon from the air into an organic molecule. Then the cycle uses the ATP and the NADPH that were made in the light-dependent reactions to convert those molecules into a three-carbon sugar called G3P. Some of that G3P leaves the cycle and is used to build glucose and other carbohydrates. The rest is recycled to regenerate RuBP, so the cycle can keep going. So the Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH to turn carbon dioxide into sugar, and it depends on the first stage for that energy.

Fun fact, rubisco is probably the most abundant protein on Earth. There is a lot of it, partly because it is actually quite slow and inefficient. Plants make huge amounts of it to compensate.

Let's pause and connect the two stages, because students often treat them as separate. The light-dependent reactions make ATP and NADPH. The Calvin cycle spends ATP and NADPH. And the Calvin cycle then returns ADP and NADP plus back to the light reactions, to be recharged. So the two stages are linked like a battery and a device: one charges, the other spends. If you shut off the light, the light reactions stop, and soon the Calvin cycle stops too, because it runs out of ATP and NADPH. That is why the second stage is called light-independent, but it is not really independent in practice.

Now, the last topic for today: what limits the rate of photosynthesis? There are three main limiting factors. The first is light intensity. More light means faster light reactions, up to a point. The second is carbon dioxide concentration. More carbon dioxide means the Calvin cycle can fix carbon faster, again up to a point. The third is temperature, because the Calvin cycle depends on enzymes like rubisco, and enzymes work best within a certain temperature range. If it gets too hot, the enzymes stop working well. The important idea is that the rate is controlled by whichever factor is in shortest supply. That is what we mean by a limiting factor. Adding more of something that is not limiting will not speed things up.

So for example, a greenhouse farmer on a bright sunny day might add extra carbon dioxide, because light is plentiful, but carbon dioxide has become the limiting factor. On a cloudy day, adding carbon dioxide would not help much, because light is the limiting factor.

Okay. Let me quickly go back to the light-dependent reactions for a second, because I saw some confused faces earlier. Remember, the oxygen released by plants comes from splitting water in the thylakoid membranes. It does not come from carbon dioxide. The carbon dioxide goes into the Calvin cycle and ends up in the sugar. Water gives the electrons and the oxygen. Carbon dioxide gives the carbon. If you remember that one distinction, you will avoid the most common exam mistake on this topic.
