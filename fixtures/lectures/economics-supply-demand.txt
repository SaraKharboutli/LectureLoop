Today's topic is the foundation of almost everything we will do this semester: supply and demand. If you understand today's lecture really well, the rest of the course gets much easier. So stay with me.

Let's start with demand. Demand describes how much of a good buyers are willing and able to purchase at different prices. The key idea is the law of demand. The law of demand says that, all else being equal, when the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls. And when the price falls, the quantity demanded rises. So price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions. That is why the demand curve slopes downward when we draw price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. Why does this happen? Partly because when something gets more expensive, people switch to substitutes, and partly because their money simply does not stretch as far. So, the law of demand: higher price, lower quantity demanded.

I always think of coffee here. When my favorite café raised the price of a latte to seven dollars, I started making coffee at home. I did not stop drinking coffee. I just bought fewer lattes. That's the law of demand in my own kitchen.

Now let's look at the other side of the market, supply. Supply describes how much of a good sellers are willing and able to offer at different prices. The law of supply says that, all else being equal, when the price rises, the quantity supplied rises too. Price and quantity supplied move in the same direction. The reason is that a higher price makes production more profitable, so existing firms produce more, and new firms may enter the market. So the supply curve slopes upward. Higher price, higher quantity supplied.

Okay, now the most important idea of the day: market equilibrium. When we put the demand curve and the supply curve on the same graph, they cross at one point. That crossing point is the equilibrium. At the equilibrium price, the quantity that buyers want to buy is exactly equal to the quantity that sellers want to sell. There is no pressure for the price to change. What happens if the price is above equilibrium? Then quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded, and we have a surplus. Sellers have unsold goods, so they cut prices, and the price falls back toward equilibrium. What if the price is below equilibrium? Then quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied, and we have a shortage. Buyers compete for limited goods, so the price is pushed up. So surpluses push prices down, shortages push prices up, and the market moves toward the equilibrium where they balance.

Now, here is where most students lose points on exams, so pay attention. There is a difference between a movement along a curve and a shift of the whole curve. A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the demand curve. We call that a change in quantity demanded. But a change in anything else, like income, tastes, or the price of a related good, shifts the entire demand curve. We call that a change in demand. For example, if people's incomes rise and they want more restaurant meals at every price, the whole demand curve for restaurant meals shifts to the right. So: own price, movement along the curve. Other factors, shift of the curve.

Let me give you one more example to make that concrete. Suppose a study comes out saying blueberries are incredibly healthy. Tastes change. At every price, people now want more blueberries. The demand curve shifts right, and the new equilibrium has both a higher price and a higher quantity. Notice the price of blueberries did not cause that. The shift happened first, and the price changed as a result.

Okay. Last topic for today, and it's a practical one: price controls. Sometimes governments do not let the market reach equilibrium. A price ceiling is a legal maximum price. If the ceiling is set below the equilibrium price, it creates a shortage, because at that low price people want to buy more than sellers want to sell. Rent control is the classic example: it can lead to a shortage of apartments. A price floor is a legal minimum price. If the floor is set above the equilibrium price, it creates a surplus. The minimum wage is often discussed as an example of a price floor in the labor market, where the surplus would be workers who want jobs but cannot find them. The key idea: a binding ceiling causes a shortage, and a binding floor causes a surplus.

Before we finish, let me come back to the law of demand for a moment, because a few of you asked me after last class about it. Remember, the law of demand is about quantity demanded responding to the good's own price, holding everything else constant. When the price goes up, buyers purchase less of that good. It is a movement along the same downward-sloping curve. It is not a shift. If you mix those up, you will get the graph questions wrong.
