So the basic story you probably know is that in 1989 the communist governments in Eastern Europe fell one after another, Poland, Hungary, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, and the Berlin Wall came down in November, and then in 1991 the Soviet Union itself dissolved into fifteen separate countries, and that's usually taken as the end of the Cold War, the end of this long standoff between the United States and the Soviet Union that had shaped basically everything since 1945. And one of the things that made the whole thing so surprising was how peaceful it was, mostly, because people had expected that if the Soviet bloc ever collapsed it would be in a war, maybe a nuclear war, and instead it happened largely through protests and negotiations and governments just losing their nerve.

Now why did it happen? Historians argue about this endlessly, but there are a few factors that come up again and again. One is the economy, the Soviet planned economy had been stagnating since the seventies, it just couldn't produce the consumer goods people wanted, and it couldn't keep up with Western technology. Another is Mikhail Gorbachev, who came to power in 1985 and introduced glasnost, which means openness, letting people criticize the system more freely, and perestroika, which means restructuring of the economy. And the crucial thing about Gorbachev is that when the Eastern European countries started to break away in 1989, he chose not to send in the tanks, which is exactly what Moscow had done in Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968. So that decision not to use force is a big part of why it was peaceful.

In 1989 an American political scientist named Francis Fukuyama wrote an essay called The End of History, and his argument was not that events would stop happening, people often get that wrong, his argument was that liberal democracy combined with market capitalism had won the big ideological contest, that there was no serious alternative left, and so the long story of ideological conflict was over. And this was hugely influential in the nineties, you see it in how Western governments talked about spreading democracy and free markets, the assumption was that every country would eventually converge on that model.

But then of course there were critics, and the most famous response came from Samuel Huntington, who argued in the Clash of Civilizations that conflict wouldn't end, it would just shift, from conflicts between ideologies to conflicts between cultures or civilizations, defined largely by religion and history. So where Fukuyama saw convergence, Huntington saw new kinds of division. And you can argue about whether either of them was right, and we will, in the seminar, but it's useful to see these as two competing predictions about the post Cold War world.

Then there's the economic side of it, which is sometimes called the Washington Consensus, a set of policies that institutions like the IMF and the World Bank pushed on developing and post communist countries, things like privatizing state companies, cutting government deficits, and opening up to trade. And in Russia in particular this was done very fast, what people called shock therapy, and the result was that a small group of people bought up huge state industries very cheaply and became extremely rich, the so called oligarchs, while a lot of ordinary people saw their savings wiped out by inflation. So when we talk about why some Russians later became disillusioned with Western style reform, this experience of the nineties is a big part of the explanation.
